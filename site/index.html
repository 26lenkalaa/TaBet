TaBet gives a probability for each Premier League match ending in a home win, a draw or an away win. Three models are in use. Each one builds on the last, and each step makes the probabilities a little more accurate.

None of them beats the betting market yet. The best one, which also knows the starting lineups, closes about half the gap between our simplest model and the market.

How each model works

Open a model to see what it does, what it uses, and when it's the one we trust.

V0: results only (Dixon-Coles)

Every team gets an attack rating and a defence rating, learned from past scores. The model predicts how many goals each side should score, then turns those two numbers into home, draw and away probabilities.

home goals ~ Poisson( exp(base + home edge + attack[home] − defence[away]) )
away goals ~ Poisson( exp(base + attack[away] − defence[home]) )
  • Recent matches count more. A match from six months ago counts half as much as one from last weekend.
  • Low scores get a correction. Real games end 0-0 and 1-1 more often than two independent goal counts suggest, so the model nudges those scorelines.
  • Ratings are pulled toward average, so a team with a few lucky results doesn't get an extreme rating.
Uses
Final scores only
Refitted
Before every matchday, on results known by then
Weakness
A lucky 3-0 counts the same as a deserved one
V1x: chances created (V0 + opponent-adjusted xG)

Goals are noisy. Expected goals (xG) measure the quality of the chances a team created, which says more about how good it really is. V1x adds two numbers to V0's prediction: how much xG a team creates, and how much its opponent usually allows.

Both are opponent-adjusted. Two expected goals against the league's best defence count for more than two against the worst. The adjustment is fitted like V0, but on xG instead of goals, with a shorter memory of about three months.

log(rate) = log(V0 rate) + b0 + b1·(team's xG attack) + b2·(opponent's xG allowed)
Uses
Scores and team xG from Fantasy Premier League
Gain over V0
−0.0031 log-loss on 2024-26, and the same direction in every season
When used
Any time before team news. This is the default early forecast.
V2x: lineups (V1x + starting-XI strength)

Team ratings assume a team's usual players. V2x checks who is actually starting. Each outfield player has a rating: their recent xG plus assists-xG per 90 minutes, pulled toward the average for their position until they've played enough to trust.

The model compares tonight's XI with the team's usual XI. If the starters are weaker than usual, it expects fewer goals. It also checks whether the opponent's defence is its regular back line.

XI strength = log( tonight's starters' rating / usual starters' rating )
Uses
Everything V1x uses, plus the confirmed starting XIs (about an hour before kickoff)
Gain over V1x
−0.0038 on 2024-26, but none on 2022-24
Where it helps
Matches where a team's lineup is far from normal, such as heavy rotation or several injuries. Elsewhere it changes almost nothing.
🔁

Rechecked on older seasons. We rebuilt the same lineup feature for 2018-21, a period never used for any decision, with player ratings based on FPL threat and creativity because xG didn't exist yet. Across all matches the gain was small (−0.0009) and not significant. In the third of matches with the most unusual lineups it was −0.0042, the same pattern as recently.

Predicted lineups (V2x a day before kickoff)

Confirmed lineups arrive an hour before kickoff. To forecast a day earlier, we feed V2x the lineups that two fantasy-football sites predict, Fantasy Football Scout and RotoWire, plus our own guess from recent team selections and FPL injury news.

  • Several versions are logged for every match, 24 hours before kickoff: V1x alone, each site's lineup, their average, and our own guess.
  • A blend is learned, not assumed. After gameweek 19 we fit how much weight the predicted lineups deserve compared with V1x alone, then freeze that weight.
  • The rules were fixed before the first forecast, so the result can't be tuned after the fact. A first read comes after gameweek 29, and the real verdict at the end of the season.
Betting odds (the benchmark, never an input)

Bookmakers' prices, with their margin removed, are the strongest public forecast. They include everything the market knows: injuries, team news, money from well-informed bettors. We use them only to measure how far we are from the best available forecast. No model ever sees them, so a model can't simply copy the market.

How a forecast is made

  1. Take every result known at least two and a half hours before the first kickoff of the day.
  2. Refit the team ratings (V0) on those results.
  3. Add the xG adjustment (V1x), and the lineup adjustment if lineups are known (V2x).
  4. Turn the two expected goal counts into a grid of scorelines, then add up the home-win, draw and away-win cells.
  5. Log the probabilities before kickoff, and score them once the result is in.

What the dropped ideas taught us
  • Team strength changes slowly. Every faster-adapting version lost. Ratings that react quickly mostly chase noise.
  • A star signing shows up in the results anyway. The new-signing ratings did pick out the big names, such as Wirtz, Cherki and Ekitiké. But a regular starter is part of the team's usual lineup, so the lineup comparison barely moves. The team's own xG catches the improvement within a few matches.
  • Stats from the same match describe the game, not the teams. Teams that are winning stop passing and start defending, so possession looks bad and tackles look good. Only stats from earlier matches can predict anything.
  • Three promoted teams a year is too few to learn how Championship form carries over.

Nothing replaces the current model yet. The bivariate Poisson model was clearly better on 2018-21 but slightly worse on 2024-26, so we're treating it as a lead to confirm, not a win. The fixes aimed at promoted teams made things worse on the clean seasons, even for promoted teams' early matches. Plugged into the lineup model (V2x), none of them changed 2024-26 by more than 0.002 either way.

How each alternative works
  • Bivariate Poisson goals. Our model treats the two teams' goal counts as independent, apart from a small fix for low scores. This version adds a shared part, so in games that open up both sides tend to score more. The team ratings are unchanged.
  • Negative binomial goals. Allows more spread in goals than a Poisson count. The data pushed it straight back to Poisson, so it changed nothing.
  • Returning-team reset. A club that comes back after a season or more away (Burnley, Leeds, Ipswich) gets a fresh rating instead of its old one from a different squad.
  • Reset + promoted-team start. As above, but every promoted side starts from how promoted teams have usually done, rather than league average.
  • Bayesian Dixon-Coles. Keeps track of how uncertain each rating is, pulls promoted teams' early ratings harder toward a promoted-team average so a few results can't swing them, and averages forecasts over that uncertainty.

Reading the numbers

Log-loss scores the probability a model gave to what actually happened. If the model said 50% and the home team won, the score is −log(0.5) ≈ 0.69. If it said 20% and the home team won, the score is −log(0.2) ≈ 1.61. Confident wrong answers cost the most. Lower is better.

Differences look tiny because football is mostly luck. Giving each outcome a one-in-three chance scores 1.10. The whole gap between our simplest model and the betting market is 0.015, so a change of 0.003 is a fifth of that gap.

We judge models on probabilities, not on picking winners. A model that says 60% should be right about six times in ten, no more and no less.

Rules every model follows

  • No peeking. A forecast only uses information that existed before kickoff. A result counts as known two and a half hours after its own kickoff.
  • No odds as inputs. Betting prices and fantasy-football ownership are benchmarks only.
  • Past predicts future, never the reverse. Models are trained on earlier matches and scored on later ones, never on a random shuffle.
  • Tests are written down first. The main comparisons were committed to the project's history before they were run.
